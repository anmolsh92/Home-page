Events, early-stage

Stop networking. Start listening.

Events are the most inefficiently used GTM channel for early-stage companies. Use them to collect frank market feedback, not to impress everyone in the room.

Events are expensive, and they're really hard to attribute. Try drawing a clean line from a booth to revenue and you'll see what I mean. A big company can live with that, because brand recall is a goal in itself. For an early-stage company it's a tougher question. If the money doesn't come back in a way you can measure, what are you actually getting out of being there?

Most people would say networking, partnerships and, if you're big enough, brand recall. All fair. But for a small team I think it makes more sense to go after smaller, incremental gains. And to make sure the whole thing doesn't end up as a photo op exercise with a couple of LinkedIn posts to show for it.

1Start in the audience

Before you spend money on a booth, go as part of the audience. Pick the events that cost you nothing, or close to nothing, and just watch how others do it.

Be choosy about which ones. Big generic events, or ones built around a broad function or industry, tend to blur together. You walk past a hundred stalls and learn very little about your own market. Niche events are much more useful. Think of events for AI marketers, or ones on AI in content and branding. The room is full of people selling to the same buyer as you, and some of those buyers are in the room too.

Sit in on the talks, walk the floor, catch the demos. Watch how others pitch and how they position their products and services, what they lead with and what they skip over quickly. You'll get a feel for the category faster than desk research will give you.

Then

Take those learnings back and use them to plan your GTM and product roadmap. Only after that should you plan your own booth, free or paid. By then you know which rooms are worth being in, and what you want to say once you're there.

2Treat it as a reality check

Once you do show up as a participant, the most useful way to use an event is as a reality check on the business. You meet peer founders who are in the same boat as you. You see how others are going about product building and GTM, up close and without the polish of a case study.

If you're honest and frank with them, a bond forms almost right away. Founders, especially in India, crave feedback, and they rarely get it straight. Customers are polite. Friends and ex-colleagues are kind. Even investors often have their reasons to hold back. So when one founder tells another that a part of their pitch isn't landing, it usually gets taken as a favour.

3Make it trackable

The catch is that these conversations can easily stay feel-good and intellectual. You have a great chat over coffee and nothing changes on Monday. So we tracked it. This is roughly what we followed across the few events we picked.

  1. 1

    Go in with 3 to 5 clearly written hypotheses or assumptions you want peers or industry leaders to weigh in on. Write them down internally before you leave.

  2. 2

    Ask peers for frank, no-filter feedback on your product or branding, and keep a record of it, including the bits that sting.

  3. 3

    Watch how other players run their demos, pitch and communicate. Note what works, and what makes people drift off.

  4. 4

    Ask veterans for the long-term view on live questions. For us that meant things like the build vs buy debate for vertical and horizontal AI, or whether marketing teams are building in-house creative teams or sticking with agency partners.

Why it matters

Secondary research usually won't give you these reality-check nuances. A report tells you what the market looks like. The founder at the next table tells you what it's actually like to sell into it.

4Count, then act

By the end of the event, the goal is to count how many responses and insights we've gathered against each of these. Then we take them back to the team and use them to brainstorm the GTM and product roadmap.

For an early-stage company, an event has paid off when you walk out with fewer untested assumptions than you walked in with.
